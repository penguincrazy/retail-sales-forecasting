01 / RESEARCH QUESTION & SCOPE

What are we trying to forecast?

Given historical daily sales and information known at the forecast origin, how accurately can we estimate sales for each store–product-family series over the next 16 days?

PREDICTION UNIT

Store × family × date

Each observation represents one product family sold by one store on one day. The target is non-negative daily sales.

TARGET TRANSFORM

log1p(sales)

Models learn on a log-transformed target to handle the highly skewed sales distribution. Predictions are returned to the sales scale before metric calculation.

DECISION BOUNDARY

No future actual sales

Sales lags and rolling features are shifted by at least 16 days. Calendar variables are known in advance; promotions are assumed known for the forecast window.

03 / PREPROCESSING & STATISTICAL REASONING

Transformations and features follow the data and forecast origin.

Preprocessing was designed around two properties of this task: daily sales are highly uneven across store–family series, and every prediction must use only information available before its 16-day forecast window.

DATA QUALITY

Panel checks before modelling

The training panel contains 3,000,888 daily observations from 1 January 2013 to 15 August 2017, covering 54 stores and 33 product families. The inspection checks required columns, missing values, negative and zero sales, and duplicate date–store–family keys. No negative sales or duplicate training keys were found; about 31.3% of observations are zero.

STATISTICAL SUMMARIES

Sales scale and variation

The notebook plots aggregate daily sales and average sales by weekday to inspect temporal patterns. It also summarises family-level counts, means, medians and standard deviations to expose differences in scale and variability before model fitting.

DISTRIBUTION

Why use log1p?

Daily sales are non-negative, contain many zeros and have a long right tail. The target is therefore transformed as log1p(sales): the transform is defined at zero and compresses large values so that extreme-volume observations have less leverage on the fitted scale. Predictions are returned with expm1 and clipped at zero before evaluation.

The 99th-percentile cap in the raw-sales histogram is visual only; it does not remove or cap observations in the modelling data.

01 · CALENDAR FEATURES

Represent recurring time structure

From each date, the pipeline extracts day of week, day of month, month, ISO week, quarter, year and a weekend indicator. Weekday and month are also represented with sine/cosine pairs so dates near the end and beginning of a cycle remain close numerically. This gives the models both readable calendar fields and smooth cyclic representations.

Feature columns: dow, day, month, week, quarter, year, weekend, dow_sin, dow_cos, month_sin and month_cos.

02 · SALES-HISTORY FEATURES

Preserve weekly patterns without seeing the horizon

For each store–family series, 52 log-sales lags are constructed at 7-day intervals from 16 through 373 days. Shifted rolling means and standard deviations are added over 7-, 14- and 28-observation windows, with at least three observations required. Both rolling statistics are shifted by the full 16-day horizon before the window is calculated.

The minimum 16-day shift ensures that even the latest source observation used by a forecast-date feature is no later than the forecast origin.

03 · BUSINESS CONTEXT

Join information available at prediction time

Store metadata (city, state, store type and cluster) is joined by store identifier using a many-to-one validation. Holiday events are converted into national, regional and local flags matched to date and the relevant geography; transferred events are excluded from the holiday flags. Missing flag matches are set to zero. Promotion counts are retained and also transformed as log1p(onpromotion).

Promotion values in the supplied 16-day test period are assumed to be known in advance. Future actual sales, oil prices and transaction counts are excluded.

04 · MISSING VALUES AND MODEL INPUTS

Fit learned transformations on training data only

Numeric inputs use median imputation. Categorical inputs use most-frequent imputation followed by one-hot encoding with unknown categories ignored. OLS, Ridge, Lasso and KNN also use standard scaling; Decision Tree, Random Forest and LightGBM use unscaled numeric inputs. Lag warm-up rows with incomplete features are excluded from model fitting, while the pipeline retains imputation for missing validation inputs.

The learned medians, category modes, one-hot levels and scaling statistics are fitted inside each fold's training pipeline—not on the validation or final holdout rows.

04 / BASELINE & MODELS

Start with a Seasonal naive (Base model) benchmark.

The baseline retrieves the latest available value for the matching weekday at or before the forecast origin. If that history is missing, it falls back to the store-family median.

BASELINE

Seasonal naive (Base model)

For each target date, the source date is shifted backward by 7-day multiples according to its horizon position. This is a meaningful benchmark for weekly demand patterns.

CANDIDATE MODELS

Eight approaches compared

Seasonal naive (Base model), OLS, Ridge, Lasso, Decision Tree, Random Forest, KNN and LightGBM. The sklearn pipelines fit preprocessing on training data only.

06 / ERROR ANALYSIS & LIMITATIONS

Aggregate performance hides uneven difficulty.

School and Office Supplies is the most difficult family by holdout RMSLE (1.09808), while Books is the lowest (0.15227). Error levels differ substantially by family and sales scale.

FEATURE ABLATION

Sales history matters most

Removing sales-history features increases mean rolling RMSLE from 0.41428 to 0.58348. This is a predictive ablation, not a causal claim.

KNOWN-FUTURE INPUT

Promotion availability

The forecast assumes promotion values are known over the 16-day horizon. In a real deployment, late changes to promotion plans could alter accuracy.

GENERALISATION

Historical and sampled setup

Results describe one retailer, one historical period and an 80,000-row fitting cap. They do not guarantee future operational performance.

07 / CONCLUSION

What the experiment supports.

Among the evaluated configurations, LightGBM has the lowest mean rolling-validation RMSLE and the lowest final-holdout RMSLE. It also improves on the base model on both final-holdout metrics. The base model has lower mean rolling-validation MAE, so the preferred model depends on the metric and evaluation period. These results support LightGBM for this defined 16-day task, not a claim of universal superiority or a complete inventory policy.

Interpretation: the evidence is strongest for lower relative/log-scale error under RMSLE. The training-row cap, promotion-availability assumption, three validation folds and historical single-retailer scope remain important limitations.
