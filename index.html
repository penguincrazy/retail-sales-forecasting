01 / RESEARCH QUESTION & SCOPE

What are we trying to forecast?

Given historical daily sales and information known at the forecast origin, how accurately can we estimate sales for each store–product-family series over the next 16 days?

PREDICTION UNIT

Store × family × date

Each row is one product family sold by one store on one day. With 54 stores and 33 families, the panel contains 1,782 store–family series; each series is forecast for the next 16 daily dates.

TARGET TRANSFORM

log1p(sales)

Models learn on a log-transformed target to handle the highly skewed sales distribution. Predictions are returned to the sales scale before metric calculation.

DECISION BOUNDARY

No future actual sales

Sales lags and rolling features are shifted by at least 16 days. Calendar variables are known in advance; promotions are assumed known for the forecast window.

03 / PREPROCESSING & STATISTICAL REASONING

Transformations and features follow the data and forecast origin.

Preprocessing was designed around two properties of this task: daily sales are highly uneven across store–family series, and every prediction must use only information available before its 16-day forecast window.

DATA QUALITY

Panel checks before modelling

The training panel contains 3,000,888 daily observations from 1 January 2013 to 15 August 2017, covering 54 stores and 33 product families. The inspection checks required columns, missing values, negative and zero sales, and duplicate date–store–family keys. No negative sales or duplicate training keys were found; about 31.3% of observations are zero.

STATISTICAL SUMMARIES

Sales scale and variation

The notebook plots aggregate daily sales and average sales by weekday to inspect temporal patterns. It also summarises family-level counts, means, medians and standard deviations to expose differences in scale and variability before model fitting.

DISTRIBUTION

Why use log1p?

Daily sales are non-negative, contain many zeros and have a long right tail. The target is therefore transformed as log1p(sales): the transform is defined at zero and compresses large values so that extreme-volume observations have less leverage on the fitted scale. Predictions are returned with expm1 and clipped at zero before evaluation.

The 99th-percentile cap in the raw-sales histogram is visual only; it does not remove or cap observations in the modelling data.

01 · CALENDAR FEATURES

Represent recurring time structure

From each date, the pipeline extracts day of week, day of month, month, ISO week, quarter, year and a weekend indicator. Weekday and month are also represented with sine/cosine pairs so dates near the end and beginning of a cycle remain close numerically. This gives the models both readable calendar fields and smooth cyclic representations.

Feature columns: dow, day, month, week, quarter, year, weekend, dow_sin, dow_cos, month_sin and month_cos.

02 · SALES-HISTORY FEATURES

Preserve weekly patterns without seeing the horizon

For each store–family series, 52 log-sales lags are constructed at 7-day intervals from 16 through 373 days. Shifted rolling means and standard deviations are added over 7-, 14- and 28-observation windows, with at least three observations required. Both rolling statistics are shifted by the full 16-day horizon before the window is calculated.

The minimum 16-day shift ensures that even the latest source observation used by a forecast-date feature is no later than the forecast origin.

03 · BUSINESS CONTEXT

Join information available at prediction time

Store metadata (city, state, store type and cluster) is joined by store identifier using a many-to-one validation. Holiday events are converted into national, regional and local flags matched to date and the relevant geography; transferred events are excluded from the holiday flags. Missing flag matches are set to zero. Promotion counts are retained and also transformed as log1p(onpromotion).

Promotion values in the supplied 16-day test period are assumed to be known in advance. Future actual sales, oil prices and transaction counts are excluded.

04 · MISSING VALUES AND MODEL INPUTS

Fit learned transformations on training data only

Numeric inputs use median imputation. Categorical inputs use most-frequent imputation followed by one-hot encoding with unknown categories ignored. OLS, Ridge, Lasso and KNN also use standard scaling; Decision Tree, Random Forest and LightGBM use unscaled numeric inputs. Lag warm-up rows with incomplete features are excluded from model fitting, while the pipeline retains imputation for missing validation inputs.

The learned medians, category modes, one-hot levels and scaling statistics are fitted inside each fold's training pipeline—not on the validation or final holdout rows.

04 / BASELINE & MODELS

Benchmark learning models against a weekly seasonal rule.

All candidates are evaluated on the same rolling-origin windows. The baseline provides a transparent reference for weekly repetition; the learned models test whether calendar, promotion, store context and sales-history features add predictive value.

REFERENCE MODEL

Seasonal naive (Base model)

For lead day h, the forecast uses the latest matching-weekday sale strictly before the forecast origin: target date minus 7 × ceil(h/7) days. If that store–family observation is absent, it uses the historical store–family median available at the origin; zero is the final fallback.

This benchmark is deliberately simple: it carries forward a recent weekly pattern rather than learning relationships between multiple features.

LINEAR & DISTANCE-BASED

OLS, Ridge, Lasso and KNN

OLS provides a linear reference. Ridge and Lasso add regularisation to control coefficient size, while KNN predicts from nearby observations in feature space. These scale-sensitive methods use standardised numeric inputs so variables measured on larger scales do not dominate distance or penalisation.

TREE-BASED

Decision Tree, Random Forest and LightGBM

The Decision Tree tests a single set of nonlinear splits; Random Forest averages many trees; LightGBM builds boosted trees sequentially. Tree models use unscaled inputs and can represent interactions among calendar, store, promotion and sales-history features.

LIGHTGBM CONFIGURATION

A fixed, reproducible comparison setup

LightGBM uses a regression objective on log1p(sales), 600 estimators, learning rate 0.04, 31 leaves, minimum child samples of 30, feature subsampling of 0.8 and L2 regularisation of 1.0. A capability check selects GPU with max_bin=63 or CPU with max_bin=255.

The settings were fixed for this experiment, not exhaustively tuned. Although subsample=0.8 is specified, subsample_freq=0 disables row bagging; feature subsampling remains active.

MODEL SELECTION

Choose on rolling RMSLE, report both metrics

Mean RMSLE across the three development folds is the selection criterion. LightGBM records 0.41407 ± 0.00659, followed by Random Forest at 0.42933. The seasonal-naive benchmark records 0.52076. MAE is also reported on the original sales scale because a model that ranks well on relative/log error need not minimise absolute sales error.

The final 31 July–15 August holdout is kept separate from this selection step and is used only for final reporting.

05 / EVALUATION & RESULTS

LightGBM leads on RMSLE.

Three consecutive rolling-origin folds each evaluate a 16-day window: 13–28 June, 29 June–14 July, and 15–30 July 2017. The separate 31 July–15 August holdout is reserved for final reporting, not model selection. RMSLE is the selection metric; MAE is reported in sales units as a complementary measure.

06 / ERROR ANALYSIS & LIMITATIONS

Where the forecasts struggle—and what the scores do not say.

Errors vary across product families, store–family series and forecast days. The aggregate score is useful for comparing models, but it does not describe every demand pattern equally well or explain why a miss occurred.

FAMILY-LEVEL ERROR

Relative error and sales-scale error tell different stories

School and Office Supplies has the highest family RMSLE (1.09808), while Books has the lowest (0.15227). But the largest family MAEs occur in high-volume groups such as Grocery I (722.38), Beverages (650.41) and Produce (384.47). A low RMSLE therefore does not necessarily mean a small error in sales units.

Each family summary contains 864 holdout observations: 54 stores × 16 dates.

FEATURE ABLATION

Sales history is the strongest feature group

Mean rolling RMSLE is 0.41428 with the full feature set. Removing sales history raises it to 0.58348; removing calendar features raises it to 0.45904. Removing promotion, store context or holidays produces smaller increases in this experiment.

Ablation measures predictive change under the tested setup. It does not prove that a feature group causes demand or that its contribution is independent of other features.

FORECAST HORIZON

Lead time does not produce a smooth error curve

Across the three rolling folds, LightGBM's daily RMSLE ranges from 0.40223 (day 1) to 0.42524 (day 16). The seasonal-naive range is 0.50415 (day 7) to 0.54239 (day 15). There is some late-horizon deterioration, but the day-by-day pattern fluctuates rather than increasing monotonically.

Each model/day summary pools 5,346 predictions (1,782 series × 3 folds). Fold variation is descriptive, not a calibrated uncertainty interval.

SERIES-LEVEL AUDIT

Aggregate metrics can conceal individual misses

The holdout audit ranks all 1,782 store–family series over their 16 forecast dates. It includes mean actual sales, mean predictions, signed error, MAE and RMSLE, allowing high-volume misses and relative-error problems to be inspected separately. This ranking identifies where errors are concentrated; it does not establish their operational cause.

KNOWN-FUTURE INPUT

Promotion values are an explicit assumption

The experiment treats promotion counts in the forecast window as known at the forecast origin. That is valid only when promotion plans are available in advance. Unannounced changes, missing plans or execution differences could change real-world performance.

LIMITATIONS

Boundaries on how to interpret the result.

These are limits of the experiment and its evidence, not just caveats about the website.

SCOPE

One retailer and historical period

The experiment uses one retailer's historical data and one final holdout window. Changes in stores, product mix, pricing, economic conditions or customer behaviour may reduce accuracy in a different period or retailer.

COMPUTE & TUNING

Fitting cap and fixed settings

Model fitting is capped at 80,000 sampled rows where required. Candidate settings were fixed rather than exhaustively tuned. Full-data retraining or a different tuning budget could change both scores and model rankings.

STATISTICAL EVIDENCE

Descriptive comparisons, not significance tests

The three rolling folds and their standard deviations describe observed variation in this evaluation. The project does not run formal significance tests, estimate calibrated prediction intervals or guarantee error bounds for future dates.

Deployment boundary: the promotion-availability assumption must be checked in a live setting. The website reads saved outputs; it does not contain a serialized LightGBM model or perform new live inference. The Forecast Explorer shows a small preview selected from the holdout prediction sample, not the full 28,512-row competition test set.
07 / CONCLUSION

What the experiment supports.

Among the evaluated configurations, LightGBM has the lowest mean rolling-validation RMSLE and the lowest final-holdout RMSLE. It also improves on the base model on both final-holdout metrics. The base model has lower mean rolling-validation MAE, so the preferred model depends on the metric and evaluation period. These results support LightGBM for this defined 16-day task, not a claim of universal superiority or a complete inventory policy.

Interpretation: the evidence is strongest for lower relative/log-scale error under RMSLE. The training-row cap, promotion-availability assumption, three validation folds and historical single-retailer scope remain important limitations.
