01 / THE QUESTION

Demand is not random.
But it is not simple.

Retail sales vary with weekly routines, seasonal cycles, promotions, local context and product-specific behaviour. A useful forecasting method must respect when a prediction is made.

How accurately can we forecast daily sales for each store–product-family pair over a 16-day horizon?

We compare a seasonal-naive baseline with statistical and machine-learning models, then investigate which feature groups contribute most to predictive performance.

03 / MODELLING APPROACH

Build features around
the forecast origin.

We frame forecasting as a 16-day information-boundary problem. Each validation fold simulates a forecast origin, and the final holdout is kept separate from model selection.

01

Temporal features

Day, weekday, month, week, quarter, year, weekend indicators and cyclical encodings.

02

Sales history

Lags from 16 to 373 days and shifted rolling means / standard deviations, computed without future target leakage.

03

Store and family context, promotion flags, and holiday types (national, regional and local).

04 / EVALUATION DESIGN

Measure errors
that matter.

We report Root Mean Squared Logarithmic Error (RMSLE) and Mean Absolute Error (MAE). Lower values indicate better predictions for both metrics.

Relative error emphasis

Compares logarithms of actual and predicted sales. It reduces the dominance of very large sales values and penalizes proportional errors.

Absolute sales-unit error

Average absolute difference between actual and predicted sales, expressed in sales units. Large absolute misses can dominate.

↳

Why both? RMSLE and MAE provide complementary views: relative/log-scale accuracy and absolute sales-scale error. We interpret them together rather than treating them as interchangeable.

06 / ERRORS, LIMITATIONS & SCOPE

Good forecasts still
miss the details.

Aggregate scores conceal variation across product families, stores and sales scales. Results should be interpreted within the boundaries of this experiment.

↗

Uneven family difficulty

School and Office Supplies has the highest family-level holdout RMSLE (1.09808), while Books has the lowest (0.15227).

≈

Metrics are complementary

LightGBM has lower holdout MAE (85.77) than seasonal naive (96.54), as well as lower RMSLE. Both metrics favour LightGBM on this holdout.

◷

Known-in-advance assumption

Promotion values are assumed available throughout the forecast horizon. Unplanned promotion changes could reduce deployed performance.

▦

Sampled model fitting

Training was capped at 80,000 rows. A different sample or full-data fit could change scores and rankings.

⌁

Historical setting

The final holdout is from July–August 2017. Results do not guarantee performance under later market conditions or changing behaviour.

!

Prediction is not causation

Ablation indicates predictive utility in this setup; it does not show that changing promotions, holidays or store context causes sales to change.

↳

Scope. The competition test set is not used for model tuning here. The site reports the internal holdout and rolling-validation results exported by the executed notebook.
