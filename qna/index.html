PROBLEM & DATA

1. What is the research question and prediction unit?

We study whether calendar, promotion, store/family context and historical sales features can accurately forecast daily sales over a 16-day horizon. One row represents one store × product family × date. The target is daily sales.

2. What dataset did we use, and what is its scale?

We use Kaggle's Store Sales – Time Series Forecasting data for Corporación Favorita in Ecuador. The training panel has 3,000,888 rows, the competition test file has 28,512 rows, and the panel spans 54 stores and 33 product families.

3. Why keep zero-sales rows?

Zero sales are valid outcomes: a product family can have no sales at a store on a particular day. They are not missing targets. About 31.3% of training rows have zero sales, so dropping them would change the forecasting problem and bias the panel toward active sales.

4. Why exclude the row identifier?

The identifier is an index-like field tied to the competition's row ordering, not a stable business feature. It could act as a proxy for time or ordering and encourage spurious fit. We use date and legitimate context directly instead.

5. Why transform sales using log1p?

Sales are highly right-skewed, with both zeros and very large values. log1p is defined at zero and compresses the scale. Models are trained on the transformed target, then predictions are converted back with expm1 and clipped to non-negative sales for evaluation.

LEAKAGE & FEATURE ENGINEERING

6. How do we respect the 16-day information boundary?

At the forecast origin, actual sales inside the next 16 days are unknown. Therefore, sales lags begin at 16 days and rolling statistics are shifted by 16 days before calculation. The target date's features cannot use sales from the forecast window.

7. Which feature groups are included?

Calendar features include weekday, day, month, week, quarter, year, weekend and cyclical encodings. Promotion features include promotion counts and a log transform. Holiday flags separate national, regional and local events. Sales history includes lags from 16 to 373 days and shifted rolling mean/standard deviation features. Store/family context is also included.

8. Why exclude future oil prices and transactions?

We could not establish that those values would be available for all dates in the 16-day prediction horizon under the evaluation setup. Excluding them avoids relying on uncertain future information. Future actual sales are never used as predictors.

9. What does the promotion assumption mean?

The notebook assumes the test-period promotion values are known in advance because the competition provides them for the forecast period. This is an explicit information-availability assumption; unplanned or revised promotions in a real deployment could reduce accuracy.

10. How is categorical and numerical preprocessing performed?

Numerical columns use median imputation and, for scale-sensitive estimators, standardisation. Categorical columns use most-frequent imputation and one-hot encoding with unseen categories ignored. The preprocessing pipeline is fitted only on the training partition within each fold.

VALIDATION & MODEL CHOICE

11. Why use rolling-origin validation instead of random K-fold?

Random K-fold would mix past and future dates and could let future patterns inform training for earlier validation observations. Rolling-origin validation trains only on dates before the next 16-day validation block, matching the temporal forecasting task more closely.

12. What are the validation and final holdout dates?

The three rolling folds are 13–28 June 2017, 29 June–14 July 2017, and 15–30 July 2017. The separate final holdout is 31 July–15 August 2017. Model selection is based on mean rolling RMSLE; the final holdout is used afterward for comparison.

13. How does the seasonal-naive baseline work?

For each target date, it finds the latest historical value on the matching weekday that is available at or before the forecast origin. The source date is moved back by a 7-day multiple based on the target's horizon position. If that historical record is missing, the store-family historical median is used.

14. Which candidate models are compared, and why LightGBM?

We compare OLS, Ridge, Lasso, Decision Tree, Random Forest, KNN and LightGBM against seasonal naive. LightGBM has the lowest mean rolling RMSLE (0.41407 ± 0.00659) and the lowest final-holdout RMSLE (0.45270) among the evaluated approaches.

15. Why report both RMSLE and MAE?

RMSLE compares log-transformed actual and predicted sales and emphasises proportional errors across scales. MAE measures average absolute error in sales units. They answer different questions. Notably, seasonal naive has lower mean rolling MAE than LightGBM, but LightGBM has better rolling RMSLE and wins both metrics on the final holdout.

RESULTS, INTERPRETATION & LIMITATIONS

16. What does feature ablation tell us?

Removing sales-history features raises mean rolling RMSLE from 0.41428 to 0.58348, the largest deterioration. Removing calendar features raises it to 0.45904. Removing holiday flags changes the score only slightly. This indicates predictive usefulness in this experiment; it does not prove causal effects.

17. Which product families are hardest to forecast?

School and Office Supplies has the largest final-holdout family RMSLE (1.09808), followed by Lingerie (0.64526) and Grocery II (0.63318). Books has the smallest family RMSLE (0.15227). Family difficulty and sales scale differ, so both RMSLE and MAE are useful context.

18. What limitations should we acknowledge?
  • The dataset covers one retailer and a historical period, so results may not generalise to other retailers or future regimes.
  • Model fitting is capped at 80,000 training rows for the relevant estimators; full-data fitting could change results.
  • Promotion values are assumed known throughout the forecast horizon.
  • Statistical significance tests were not part of this experiment; fold standard deviations are descriptive.
  • Feature ablation is not causal analysis.
